Part one

What we already agree on

A fair amount got settled in the smaller catch-ups since this was first written. Worth reading out loud at the start so we don't spend the session re-arguing it.

  • We're both partners. Not a website Alana builds and Kim pays a percentage on. Shared responsibility for the business as a whole. The original 30% framing is superseded and the revenue share is off the table.
  • Alana leads the technical side, Kim leads marketing and is the face of the business. Alana picks up maintenance and marketing work once the build settles down.
  • We're launching as Equestrian Coaches Australia. Register that business name, buy that domain, build the site there. The broader parent name can wait until there's actually a second profession.
  • A parent brand comes later, not instead. Coaches launch first. Farriers, bodyworkers, saddle fitters, dentists and trainers come later under the same roof, on the same site.
  • Riders coming back is the point. Whether they come for a coach or a farrier, the goal is the same platform every time.
  • Written properly from the start. Ownership, contributions, and what happens if someone leaves — agreed now, not patched later. Kim is taking the structure and tax questions to her accountant.
  • Stage one is not passive. Getting professionals and riders onto an empty marketplace is the hard part, and it's mostly manual.
Part two

The agenda

Shorter than it was, because several items have answers now. Timings are a guide. The split and who pays for what are the two that matter — if we run out of time, we run out of time on the small stuff, not on those.

5Minutes

Where we're going

Quick check that we're picturing the same thing in three years: how many professions, how many states, and whether the ambition is a solid side business or something we'd want to sell one day. It changes a lot of the answers below.

10Minutes

The numbers, before anything else

Walk through part three of this document. Splitting a business that costs money for a year is a different conversation from splitting one that makes money, and it's fairer to both of us to have that in the open first.

15Minutes

Put a number on what we're each committing

The roles are agreed. What's missing is the size of them. Best done by writing our own down separately first, then comparing — it stops one answer anchoring the other.

  • Hours a week, now and ongoing. Kim's number sets the pace of the whole first six months
  • Specific jobs, not categories — "sign up ten coaches a month", not "marketing"
  • The flex, written both ways. We've agreed Alana moves into marketing when the build eases. Worth writing the mirror at the same time: Kim takes on more when recruiting is the constraint. Otherwise the sentence only points one direction, and the person whose work has a natural end quietly ends up doing both jobs
20Minutes

The split, and how it's earned

Settled: Kim 70, Alana 30, on profits, losses and capital, and it's in draft 4. Every decision is unanimous, so the number carries no control with it. What's left in this slot is how a share is earned and what happens if one of us stops.

  • How a share is earned over time, so neither of us ends up owning part of a business we stopped working on. This matters whatever the number — a split with nothing attached is the one where someone who walks in month five keeps their share of what the other goes on to build. Draft 4 uses a buy-out at independent valuation for the first 24 months instead of a vesting schedule
  • Whether money in and work in are weighted the same
  • What happens if someone's contribution drops off for a while — illness, work, life
10Minutes

Who pays for what

The biggest thing still open, and the one with real invoices behind it. Roughly $7,000–9,000 across year one, with the solicitor and the domain due within weeks. Options: split it down the middle, split it in line with ownership, or one of us fronts it and gets paid back out of revenue before anything is shared.

Also worth agreeing when we each start taking money out, and what happens if one of us can't cover a bill that month. Kim's accountant can advise on whether money fronted is best treated as a loan or a capital contribution — but which of us fronts it is ours to decide.

10Minutes

Structure, and who owns what

A general partnership with a proper written agreement (around $1,500–3,000 — the one cost genuinely worth paying). ABN and business name registration. And the important one: the domain, the code and the brand are owned by the business, not by either of us personally. That's much easier to agree now than after it's built.

10Minutes

If one of us wants out

Not a pessimistic conversation — it's the one that lets us both relax. How someone exits, how their share is valued, whether the other gets first refusal, what happens if someone stops contributing without formally leaving, and what we do if someone offers to buy the whole thing.

2Minutes

The name — now the blocking one

Equestrian Coaches Australia stays, as the name of the coaching section and the name on everything we send a coach. What we still need is the name above it, because farriers are launching alongside coaches and a farrier cannot sit under a site called Equestrian Coaches Australia. The parent name now blocks both business names, both domains and Stripe. The shortlist is still in the setup document, and the reasoning is in One site, two professions.

Who does what by when

Three or four actions with names and dates on them. Chiefly: who books the solicitor, who registers the ABN, and when we next sit down.

Part three

The launch plan, in short

The full version is in The First 100 Coaches. This is the shape of it, for the ten minutes we spend on it.

The one finding that shapes everything

We can't buy coaches. We have to go and get them.

There are only about 3,000–6,000 riding coaches in Australia. That's a tiny audience, and advertising to a tiny audience is expensive — roughly $272 to acquire one coach on Facebook, against the $50 maximum a coach is worth to us. We checked what the competition does: no Australian equestrian directory, marketplace or magazine is running ads either. They're not missing a trick, the numbers just don't work.

So the first hundred coaches come from Kim's contacts, Facebook groups, events and direct approaches. Riders are the opposite — there are hundreds of thousands of them, and they arrive through Google searching "riding instructor near me". That's where the site build is pointed.

Three points where we stop and check

Built into the plan so we're never spending on autopilot. At each one we decide to keep going, change something, or stop.

  • Week 12 — 25 coach profiles live. If we can't get 25 for free, we won't get 100.
  • Month 6 — 40+ coaches and 750+ rider sessions. Proof that riders can find us.
  • Month 8 — 40% of free coaches convert to paid. The only checkpoint that really matters.
Needs a decision

Resolved. The founding offer is a date now, not a number of months. It runs free until 30 April 2027, and that date lives in the admin settings so it can move without a deploy. It lands close to the six months this page recommended, and it keeps the paid test at month eight where the checkpoints need it.

One piece left: which plan a founding coach is actually on during the free period. The old wording promised Spotlight, and the current wording says only that they pay nothing until the date.

Worth ten minutes on its own

Price is the biggest lever we have, and it costs nothing to pull. At $9.99 a month we clear $10.74 per coach after fees. At $19 we clear $18.20 — nearly double, with the same coaches, the same work and the same spend. Break-even drops from nine paying coaches to five, and from around month thirteen to month ten.

A coach charging $80–120 an hour needs one lesson every six months from the platform to justify $19. That's not a hard sell if the platform works. Suggest we test $19 with the founding group rather than $9.99.

Before the day

Three things to bring

A number for your own hours. Not "as much as I can" — an actual figure for a normal week, and an honest one. Everything in part two hangs off it.

A split you'd be happy with in three years. Not what feels polite in the room. Write it down before we sit down, and we compare. If they're close, this takes five minutes. If they're not, far better to find out now than after the agreement is drafted.

What you're each willing to put in, in cash. A figure for the first year, and whether you'd rather front it and be repaid or split every invoice as it lands. Both work. Not deciding is the only option that doesn't.
