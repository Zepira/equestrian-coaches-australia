01

How this is owned

Changed

The earlier version of this page said form a company now. That was over-engineered for the size of this business, and the partnership instinct is the better call. What follows is the revised version, including where the first one overstated the difference.

Everyone repeats the same warning about general partnerships: each partner is personally liable for the whole of the debts, including ones the other person ran up. That is true. It is also worth asking what this business could actually owe.

One correction to the earlier version. It compared a cheap handshake agreement under a sole trader against an expensive shareholders agreement under a company, which made the gap look bigger than it is. You need a proper written agreement whichever structure you pick, and it is the largest single cost either way. The structure is the cheap decision. The agreement is the one worth paying for.

Two things that make a partnership safe enough here

  • A binding limit in the agreement. Neither partner can commit the partnership beyond an agreed amount, say $200, without the other's written consent. That closes most of the "liable for debts I did not agree to" worry between two people who already trust each other. It is one clause.
  • Insurance from the first payment. Public liability and professional indemnity cover the injury-claim tail that limited liability would otherwise absorb, and they cost less than running a company. Same trigger as decision four: buy before the first coach pays.

Those two together get you most of what incorporating would have bought, for a fraction of the ongoing cost.

Converting later is not a trap

The usual reason to incorporate early is that moving assets into a company later triggers capital gains tax. The ATO's small business restructure roll-over exists precisely for this: businesses under $10 million turnover can move active assets from a partnership into a company without an immediate tax bill, as long as it is a genuine restructure and each person's economic share stays the same.

It defers rather than erases the tax, and stamp duty and accountant fees still apply, so ask the accountant what the move would actually cost before you decide. But "we will be stuck" is not a reason to incorporate now.

Suggested

A general partnership with a properly drafted agreement, a binding limit clause, insurance from the first payment, and a written note to review incorporating at month 8. Month 8 is when the founding coaches convert to paid, which is the same moment the business first has real revenue, real obligations and something worth protecting.

Spend the money on the agreement, not the structure.

The one thing that would change this

If either of you has personal assets you would be badly hurt to lose, and a broker cannot quote sensible professional indemnity cover for a directory, then limited liability is worth the $1,400 a year and you should incorporate instead.

That is a question only the two of you can answer, and it is worth answering out loud rather than assuming. Everything above assumes the answer is no.

One piece of vocabulary that still matters. A general partnership is a legal structure, and a written partnership agreement is a separate thing that sits on top of it. Without the agreement, the default rules apply — and one of those is that the partnership dissolves when a partner leaves. Make sure the agreement covers continuity, or the structure you picked for simplicity becomes the thing that ends the business when someone gets busy.

The split, and why vesting matters more

The number is now settled: Kim 70, Alana 30, on profits, losses and capital, and it is written into draft 4 of the agreement. Every decision is unanimous, so the split carries no control with it. The rest of this section is about what protects it, which is the half that still matters.

The hours are lopsided in the first year, and it is worth being honest that they will be. Alana is finishing a build; Kim is starting outreach that does not fully begin until there are profiles to point at. That question is now answered, and it did not turn on the hours: the agreed number is Kim 70 and Alana 30, on the basis that Kim's contacts and the idea are worth what they are worth regardless of who is busier in year one. Nobody raises it again in month nine.

Both contributions are necessary and neither is replaceable. Alana is building a product that would otherwise cost tens of thousands to commission. Kim brought the idea and, more importantly, is the entire go-to-market plan, because coach recruitment runs on her name and her contacts. Neither of you gets anywhere alone.

Earning your share, in one paragraph

Your split is agreed now, but the agreement says a partner who leaves early gives most of it back, with the amount they hand over shrinking as time passes. A common shape is four years with a one-year cliff: leave in the first year and you keep nothing, then you keep a growing slice each year until it is fully yours. Companies call this vesting; a partnership agreement can do exactly the same thing in its own words.

It protects both of you from the same thing. If Kim stops recruiting in month four, Alana is not left carrying the business with a silent half-owner. If Alana stops building in month four, Kim is not paying half the business to someone who left her with an unfinished site.

How Alana is paid

Settled

Ownership, not a revenue share. Kim's co-ownership offer replaced the 30% arrangement rather than sitting alongside it, and both of you are treating the build as Alana's founding contribution the same way Kim's contacts and idea are hers. The rest of this section is kept as the reasoning, and because the revenue definition in it is worth reading once even though you are not using it.

The 30% revenue share was designed for a different arrangement, where Alana was a contractor building a site for Kim's business. Now that she is a co-owner, it is replaced rather than kept alongside.

Do not stack the two

Taking 30% off the top and half of what is left means Alana receives around 65% of every dollar. Kim will spot that, and she will be right to. Pick one mechanism.

If you do keep a revenue share for any period, write the definition down in a sentence. "Revenue means money actually received into the business account from coach subscriptions, less payment processing fees, refunds and chargebacks." Paid monthly against an invoice, with the Stripe payout report attached, so nobody has to take anyone's word for the number.

Under the ownership route, neither of you should expect to take money out for a while. At 100 paying coaches the business nets about $1,074 a month, and the running costs come out of that first.

04

Who pays the bills

The main thing still open

This is now the biggest unanswered question between you, and it is worth settling before the next build phase rather than after. Kim is seeing her accountant, which is the right person for the tax half — but who fronts the money is a decision the two of you make, not one the accountant makes for you.

Running costs are about $50 a month for hosting, domain and email. The marketing budget is $1,448 in the first six months and up to $3,960 more after that if the checkpoints pass. All of it happens before there is any revenue.

Do you actually need insurance?

Not yet. The blanket "get public liability and professional indemnity before launch" was too cautious for what you are actually doing on day one, which is running a website with twenty-five free listings on it.

Public liability covers injury or damage to other people arising from your business activities. You do not run lessons, own premises the public visits, or handle horses. The usual trigger barely exists. Professional indemnity covers claims that your service caused someone financial loss, and for a directory the realistic version is a coach or rider saying you presented someone as qualified when they were not.

Which means the thing that actually protects you early is free:

  • Never say "verified", "accredited" or "recommended" about a coach unless you have genuinely checked. Say the information was supplied by the coach.
  • Terms of service that state plainly you do not vet or accredit anyone, and that arrangements are between the rider and the coach.
  • Insurance itself, once there is revenue. In a partnership it is doing the job that limited liability would otherwise do, which is exactly why decision one leans on it. See the trigger table below.
Also agree

A spending limit neither of you crosses alone. $200 is a sensible line. Under it, whoever runs that area just spends it. Over it, you both say yes first.

What each of you is committing to

The roles are agreed. What is not written down is the size of them — how much time each of you is actually putting in, and what happens when that changes. Write it in plain terms or it becomes a running argument.

Alana

Build and keep it running

Ship the remaining phases. Then bugs, security and dependency updates, hosting, small content changes, the search and SEO work, and technical and billing questions from coaches. Roughly four to six hours a month once it is live, on top of whatever new features you both agree to.

Front-loaded, and everyone knows it. As the build settles she moves hours across into maintenance and marketing.

Kim

Bring the coaches, and be the face

Personal outreach at an agreed volume, Facebook group presence, press, events, and coach-facing support. The public presence of the business is hers. The marketing plan runs on roughly three hours a week of messages.

If three hours a week is not realistic, say so now and the plan changes rather than quietly failing. This is the one number the whole first year depends on.

Write the flex both ways

You have agreed that Alana takes on marketing and maintenance work once the technical load eases. Write the mirror of it down at the same time: Kim takes on more when recruitment is the constraint.

Otherwise the sentence only points in one direction, and a year from now the person whose work has a natural end is doing both jobs. Nobody intends that; it just happens if only half of it is written.

Rough response times stop "I'll get to it" from meaning nothing. Site down: same day. Something broken but the site works: five business days. Small change or new content: two weeks.

Revisit both commitments at each of the three checkpoints in the marketing plan. Circumstances change, and a scheduled review is much easier than one person raising it out of the blue.

06

Who owns what?

One rule covers nearly all of it: every account is in the business's name, with both of you as administrators. Nothing sits in a personal account.

This is the thing that quietly wrecks small businesses. One person registers the domain on their personal card, and three years later nobody can move it, transfer it, or sell without them.

Two clauses worth writing at the same time, because they protect opposite sides. Alana keeps the right to reuse generic components of her own work in other projects — she should not be barred from ever again using a search box she wrote — while the ECA product itself belongs to the company. And Kim gets a handover clause: if Alana stops, the code, accounts and enough written notes stay with the business so another developer can pick it up.

Who does what?

08

Leaving, and selling

Nobody wants to write this while everyone is enthusiastic. It is also the only time you can write it fairly, because neither of you knows yet who will be the one wanting out.

Most of it is handled by the vesting in decision two. What vesting does not cover:

If Alana stops

Kim keeps running it

The unearned part of her share returns to the business. Full handover of code, accounts and notes, so another developer can take over. What she has earned stays hers, or the business buys it back using a valuation method you agree now.

If Kim stops

Alana keeps running it

Same in reverse. The unearned part returns, the earned part stays or is bought back. A handover of the coach relationships and the accounts, so recruitment does not simply stop.

Two more clauses worth having. A deadlock rule, since every decision is unanimous and so either of you can block the other. This is still open in draft 4, at clause 9.5: name a trusted third party to break a tie, or agree that an unresolved deadlock triggers a buy-sell. And drag-along and tag-along, which sound technical but just mean neither of you can be forced into a sale you hate, and neither can be left behind in one.

On a sale you each take your percentage of the proceeds. That is the main practical advantage of owning a share over taking a revenue share, which normally just ends when the business changes hands.

09

The name, and which domains to buy

"Equestrian Coaches Australia" says exactly what it is. That is why it works brilliantly for launch, and why it becomes a ceiling the day you add farriers and bodyworkers.

Three facts make most of this cheap to solve.

Changed

This page previously argued that the parent domain should lead, and called the rider-retention point decisive. That was overweighted, and Kim and Alana have since agreed they want the Equestrian Coaches Australia name.

Two reasons the earlier argument was weaker than it read. A rider looking for a farrier in two years will search for one the same way they searched for a coach — nobody types a directory's domain from memory for a need that comes round every few years. And the retention channel in the marketing plan is the mailing list, which moves with you whatever the domain says.

Against that, Kim's cold outreach is measurably better with an address that explains itself, and coach recruitment is the binding constraint on the entire first year.

Suggested

Register "Equestrian Coaches Australia" with ASIC, buy the matching domain, and launch there. Do not pick the parent name yet. It is not blocking anything, and there may never be a second profession to hang it on — checkpoint three at month eight could say stop.

The whole cost of this choice is one domain migration, somewhere around month 13 to 24, and only if the business is working well enough to expand. That is a good problem to have.

Six things that make a later move cheap

Almost all migration pain comes from decisions made at the start. These cost nothing now and turn a scary job into an afternoon.

  1. No "coach" in any URL path that will eventually hold every profession. Coaches at /coaches/, so /farriers/ slots in beside it.
  2. One BASE_URL constant. Every canonical tag, sitemap entry, structured-data URL and email link derives from it, so a domain change is one environment variable rather than a find-and-replace.
  3. Generate the redirect map from the database, not by hand. With slugs in Postgres it is one script over a few hundred pages.
  4. Buy the parent domain the day you pick it and 301 it to the coaches domain immediately. DNS and certificates get sorted while it is low-stakes, and on migration day you are reversing an arrow you have already tested.
  5. Verify both domains in Search Console as soon as each exists. You need both properties to run a Change of Address.
  6. Keep the brand name out of slugs entirely.

Keep the 301s running for at least a year, map every old page to its exact new equivalent rather than dumping them on the homepage, and use the Change of Address tool. Migrations that go wrong nearly always go wrong on one of those three.

How the names actually stack up

There is no separate "parent registration". The parent and each profession are the same kind of thing, all hanging off one ABN:

  • The partnership gets an ABN and a tax file number. Its legal name is simply your two names. Nothing else to register.
  • The parent name, whenever you settle on one, is a business name registered with ASIC against that same ABN. $47 a year, or $108 for three. It can be added at any time — nothing about launching now forecloses it.
  • "Equestrian Coaches Australia" is a business name against that ABN, and it is the only one you need today. "Equine Farriers Australia" later is another $47. Same tier, same price, no restructure.
A business name is not ownership

Registering a name with ASIC does not stop anyone else using it. Only a trade mark does that — $250 per class through IP Australia's picklist, ten years' protection, and around seven months to come through.

Worth knowing before the naming session, because descriptive names are the hardest to trade mark. "Equine Professionals Australia" describes a category, so it would be difficult to protect and easy for a competitor to sit next to. A distinctive parent name is cheaper to defend as well as better to remember.

Sections are real, redirects are not

/coaches and later /farriers are real sections with real pages — they are the site. Only whole domains ever redirect, and for now there is only one domain.

Do not buy a domain per profession. An unused domain has no search signals to pass on, so pointing it at the site achieves nothing and costs about $20 a year forever. One domain today, and a second only when there is a parent brand worth moving to.

On the parent name

You are right that "Equine Professionals Australia" is flat. A parent name does not have to describe what it does. Horse Deals is not called Australian Horse Classifieds, and it has held a whole marketplace for forty years.

Since the search words live in your page titles rather than your domain, the parent name is free to be short, warm and memorable instead of descriptive. That is a session for the two of you, and it is worth having before you buy, not after.

Names to argue about

A starting list, not a recommendation. The job of this table is to give the two of you something concrete to react to, because reacting is much easier than inventing. All of them come from the horse world, none of them lock you to coaching, and every one still works in the sentence "find a farrier on…".

On "Equitopia" and the whole Equi– family

It's the first place everyone goes, which is exactly why it's the most crowded corner of the market. Equitopia is already a US equine education business, Equiline is a major Italian apparel brand, Equinest is a running site, and Equitana is a huge expo that also runs in Australia.

Coined Equi– names also tend to land somewhere between a supplement brand and a software startup, which is a long way from a well-made magazine about riding. Not a rule — if one of you loves one, chase it. Just expect the clash search to come back messy.

What to avoid

  • Anything with "coach", "instructor" or "lessons". That is the ceiling you are trying to get out from under.
  • Descriptions of the category. "Equine Professionals Australia" is both forgettable and close to impossible to trade mark.
  • Anything that only works for one profession. Say it out loud followed by "…find a farrier". If that sentence is strange, the name is too narrow.

Three checks before anyone falls in love

  1. ASIC business name search — is the name available to register at all.
  2. IP Australia trade mark search — the one that can actually stop you. Someone else's registered mark beats your business name, no matter who registered first.
  3. Domain availability, and then register the business name before the .com.au. auDA allows a .com.au that matches your registered business name or trade mark, or that is a synonym for a service you provide. "Equestrian Coaches Australia" is descriptive enough that the synonym route would probably carry it on its own, but registrars apply that test inconsistently — and a coined parent name like Waler is a synonym for nothing, so it definitely needs the business name first. Cheap and easy to get the wrong way round.

Two informal ones worth doing too: search the name on Facebook, because the equestrian world lives there and a clash will be visible; and say it out loud to someone in a noisy place. If you have to spell it, it fails.

Already handled in the build

The site structure is being built to take this. Coaches sit at /coaches/, so farriers and bodyworkers become /farriers/ and /bodyworkers/ alongside them rather than needing anything rebuilt. The one rule for Alana: nothing that will eventually hold every profession gets the word "coach" in its address.

Last thing

Two questions for a professional

Kim is doing the first one

Kim is taking the structure and tax questions to her accountant. Worth sending her the list below beforehand — an accountant asked "what should we do?" gives a general answer, and one asked five specific questions gives five usable ones. The whole thing is a twenty-minute conversation if she walks in with them.

One thing to make sure she asks, because it is the one that costs money if it is missed: how the work each of you has already contributed gets treated. Alana has three quarters of a product built and Kim has an idea and a contact list, and neither has been recorded anywhere. There are sensible ways to account for that and unsensible ones, and the difference is easier to fix now than after there is revenue.

Take the filled-in table at the top into both conversations and they get short.

  • An accountant, on structure and tax. Whether a partnership is the right start at this size, what it would cost to move into a company later including stamp duty and their own fees, how to treat the work each of you has already contributed, and whether early losses can be offset against your other income (there are tests that often block this for small side businesses). Ask about superannuation too if any arrangement ends up looking like paid work rather than ownership. And ask how to handle it if one partner ends up fronting most of the costs — whether that is a loan to the partnership, a capital contribution, or something that should shift the split.
  • A solicitor, on the partnership agreement. The split, earning it over time, a binding limit so neither of you can commit the other, deadlock, exits, continuity when someone leaves, and assignment of the code and brand to the business. Ideally someone who has done founder agreements before. A template you have already filled in is a much cheaper starting point than an empty page.

Neither of you needs to register for GST until the business turns over $75,000 a year, which is around 580 paying coaches. Ask the accountant anyway, because registering earlier is sometimes worth it.

The one-sentence version

Keep the structure simple and spend the money on the agreement instead — that is where the protection actually lives, and it costs the same whichever structure sits underneath it.
